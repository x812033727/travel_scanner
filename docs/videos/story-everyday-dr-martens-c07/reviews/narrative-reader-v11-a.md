## What a first-time viewer can follow

The central question is understandable: how a boot meant to cushion a worker's foot became a visible sign of youth identity. The sequence is mostly causal: the German cushioning idea reaches an experienced British work-boot maker; the redesigned 1460 reaches working wearers; young wearers expose and polish boots as part of a recognizable outfit; later groups attach different meanings to a similar boot. The closing idea in `6.11` answers the opening question by giving agency to wearers rather than to the factory. I could follow that without outside context.

The narration has concrete images worth preserving: a cut trouser hem (`1.2`, `4.4`), a postal route (`4.1`), polished boots (`4.8`), lace colors (`6.3`–`6.5`), flower-decorated boots (`6.7`), and a patterned 1995 fashion boot (`6.9`). These keep the story about people wearing the object, rather than a list of subcultures.

## Actionable holds

### C07-NAR-V11-A-01 — Chapter 3 loses the story after the name reveal

**Rows:** `3.7`–`3.10`, especially `3.9`–`3.10`. After the satisfying date/name answer, the narration moves through an exhibition and summer orders, then twice steps outside the scene to qualify the evidence: not every later Dr. Martens boot is a 1460, and the first customer's purchase date is unknown. Those qualifications may be important, but in spoken order they stall the question viewers came for: how a work boot reached young wearers. The exhibition has no visible consequence in this version, so the chapter briefly reads like a fact-file aside.

**Fix to test:** Keep the essential model/date precision in one short spoken beat, then carry the existing work-boot image directly into the postal-worker scene of `4.1` and the young wearer's exposed hem in `4.4`. If the exhibition remains, make its narrated role in that handoff clear using already verified material. Avoid inventing a first buyer or claiming that the exhibition caused youth adoption. Re-read `3.5` through `4.5` aloud; the viewer should be able to say what changed between factory, work use, and deliberate display.

### C07-NAR-V11-A-02 — Chapter 6 changes meanings without showing the pivot

**Rows:** `6.5`–`6.8`. `6.5` finishes an unusually strong lace-color street scene. `6.6` announces another look, `6.7` introduces girls decorating boots, and `6.8` moves to music videos and fashion stores. Chronology is clear, but the connection between politically read laces and decorated/fashion boots only becomes explicit in the abstract thesis at `6.11`. On a first listen, this feels like the start of a new mini-story rather than the consequence of the same boot being reinterpreted by different wearers.

**Fix to test:** Bridge `6.5` to `6.7` with the existing visual contrast: one street reads a lace as a political signal while another wearer changes the boot's appearance with flowers. Then let the already narrated sales observation and later patterned retail boot show how that new reading becomes visible beyond the street. Preserve the distinction between Dr. Martens models; do not imply that every group wore the identical 1460 or that a documented manufacturer response caused the later fashion change unless separately verified. Re-read `5.16` through `6.11` aloud and check that each change of wearer changes the same central question, rather than merely adding a decade.
