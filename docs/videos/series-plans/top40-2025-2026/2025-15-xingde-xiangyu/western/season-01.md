# 第 1 季《Shatter》逐集細綱

主題：She loses her baby, her name and her home — then signs a marriage contract that ends the day he is sentenced.

開季：Port Merrow, the Glass House on the bluff that Grant Whitlock built for her. Nora Quinn is thirty-one, five months pregnant and high-risk; Dr. Brandt has prescribed prenatal medication. At nineteen she learned to blow glass from Walter Pell at Pell Glassworks on Kiln Row; in six years of marriage she hasn't touched a furnace. The Forever vase she blew at twenty-five as a wedding gift to her husband has become Whitlock Home's best seller, and her name isn't on the label. Her mother-in-law, Peg Whitlock, comes once a week to complain the soup is bland and the nursery window is too big. In episode 1 she dreams the Glass House shatters from the roof down, pane by pane, without a sound; a crooked glass bird falls and a hand catches it. She wakes, takes her pill, and says: “My pill tastes sweet today.”

季末：The sweet pill is explained (m01: not the medication Dr. Brandt prescribed but a lookalike sugar tablet; the hospital pharmacy pickup log shows the real pills were collected every month, signed for by Grant Whitlock). The signature on the property agreement is explained (m02: traced from the signature on her pre-marriage design sketches — every stroke hesitates and the flick at the end is missing; it is dated the night she was under anesthesia at Merrow General, and the transfer of the Forever design rights was traced by the same hand; the court-appointed examiner confirms the forgery, the court freezes the transfers of the Glass House and the accounts, and the forgery charge is filed). Why Peg left with her is explained (m03: the day Nora came home from the hospital, Peg found a confectionery receipt in her son's car — “Prop tablets, qty 100” — and overheard him on the phone, “That's taken care of”; thirty-four years ago she sat alone all night in the same hospital's hallway). Nora lost the baby in the seventh-floor hallway at Merrow General (only the hallway, a half-knitted baby hat and the flickering light at the end). The day she came home the locks were changed; attorney Leonard Fisk handed her the property agreement she had “signed,” giving Grant the house, the accounts and the Forever design rights, while Sienna Vale had workers hanging curtains in the Glass House. She took only her mother's glass block, the Keepsake, and Peg packed a suitcase and left with her. Deputy Prosecutor Marcus Reyes declined to file on her first report (“Half a pill isn't enough”). Elias Ashford handed her the three clauses outside the Harborview Motor Inn; she signed them and filed for divorce. Ninety days later, at Merrow County Superior Court, the judge entered the divorce decree — “I'll sign the divorce. The house — I'll see you in court.” — and across the hall at the County Clerk she and Elias took out a marriage licence, Peg and Bev from the motel as witnesses. Grant folded Sienna's marriage application into his pocket: “Some other day.” At the launch of the new Forever collection she used the spotlight to reveal a line of bubbles in the vase's base — her initials and their wedding date; in six years he had never once turned it over. Elias leased her the abandoned Pell Glassworks on Kiln Row; she recognized the place she had apprenticed at nineteen, and he said, “Bought it at a sheriff's sale.” Furnace No. 3 was lit for the first time in twelve years and the studio was named Remelt; the blind competition for the ferry terminal chandelier brought in the first commission; a glass-tube case tracker went up on the studio wall, its bead pushed from Report to Charges, and the first two panels went up on the evidence wall. In the last episode Grant walks into Remelt Studio for the first time, glances at the evidence wall and smiles: “Two panels of glass. Drop it, Nor. You can't beat me alone.” By the furnace, Elias turns, pulls off his heat glove: an old burn scar across the back of his left hand. Grant's smile freezes: “…You.” Elias says: “Hello, Sales. Twelve years.” Nora's blowpipe stops mid-air — only people from Pell's ever called him that.
